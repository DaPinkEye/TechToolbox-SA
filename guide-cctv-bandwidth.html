CCTV bandwidth basics

Bitrate, resolution, retention and why storage estimates vary

When sizing a CCTV system, the two biggest questions are almost always:

  1. How much network bandwidth will the cameras use?
  2. How much hard-drive space do I need for the required retention?

Both answers depend mainly on bitrate.

What is bitrate?

Bitrate is the amount of data the camera sends every second, measured in Mbps (megabits per second). Higher bitrate = better image quality and smoother motion, but more storage and more network load.

What affects bitrate?

  • Resolution – 4K uses far more data than 1080p
  • Frame rate (fps) – 25 fps uses more than 12 fps
  • Codec – H.265 is roughly 30–50% more efficient than H.264
  • Scene complexity – busy streets with lots of movement need higher bitrate than quiet corridors
  • Compression / quality setting – higher quality = higher bitrate
  • Day vs night – infrared night vision often increases bitrate

Typical bitrate ranges (real-world)

  • 2MP (1080p) H.264 – 2 to 4 Mbps
  • 2MP H.265 – 1.5 to 3 Mbps
  • 4MP / 5MP H.265 – 3 to 6 Mbps
  • 4K (8MP) H.265 – 6 to 12 Mbps (sometimes higher)
These are averages. Always check the manufacturer’s datasheet and, if possible, measure the actual bitrate on a live camera in the real environment.

How storage is calculated

The basic formula is:

Storage (GB) ≈ Number of cameras × Bitrate (Mbps) × 86400 × Days ÷ 8 ÷ 1000

Or in simpler terms: more cameras, higher bitrate or longer retention = more hard drive space needed.

Common mistakes that cause problems

  • Using the manufacturer’s “best case” bitrate instead of a realistic average
  • Forgetting that continuous recording uses far more space than motion-only
  • Not leaving headroom on the hard drive (RAID, formatting overhead, etc.)
  • Under-estimating night-time bitrate on infrared cameras
  • Ignoring the network uplink when many cameras stream to a remote NVR or VMS

Practical tip

When quoting a job, calculate storage at both the expected average bitrate and a slightly higher “worst case” bitrate. This protects you if the customer later turns up the quality settings or adds more cameras.
